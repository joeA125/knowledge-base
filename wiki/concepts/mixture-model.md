# Finite Mixture Model

A finite mixture model represents a population as a weighted combination of $K$ component distributions:

$$f(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, f_k(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_k), \qquad \sum_k \pi_k = 1, \; \pi_k > 0$$

Each observation is assumed generated by one component, with a latent indicator $\mathbf{Z}_i$ recording which. Under the standard definition (McLachlan & Peel, 2000), a **cluster is the set of observations generated by the same component** — which is what makes mixtures a *model-based* approach to clustering rather than a heuristic one.

## Why Model-Based Clustering

Distance-based methods (k-means, hierarchical clustering) require choosing a similarity metric, which is often arbitrary — and for structured data like sequences, ad hoc. Mixture models instead specify a generative process, which brings:

- **A principled cluster definition** rather than one implied by a metric.
- **Soft assignment** via posterior probabilities $r_{ik} = P(Z_{ik}=1 \mid \boldsymbol{x}_i)$, quantifying uncertainty.
- **Likelihood-based [[model-selection|model selection]]** for choosing $K$.
- **Arbitrary component distributions** — components need not be Gaussian, or even vectors.

That last point matters most. [[football-event-sequences-point-process-mixture|Amezouwui et al. (2025)]] use components that are entire marked spatio-temporal [[point-process]]es, letting them cluster **whole football possessions** — variable-length sequences of typed, timed, located events. No distance metric on such objects is obvious; a generative model sidesteps the question.

## Estimation

Fitted by maximum likelihood via the [[expectation-maximization|EM algorithm]], which alternates:

- **E-step:** compute posterior membership probabilities given current parameters.
- **M-step:** maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood.

The complete-data log-likelihood decomposes cleanly over components, which is what makes EM natural here:

$$\ell_c(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_i \sum_k Z_{ik}\left[\ln \pi_k + \ln f_k(\boldsymbol{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_k)\right]$$

## Choosing K

The number of components is not estimated by maximum likelihood — the likelihood increases monotonically with $K$. The standard remedy is a penalised criterion, most commonly BIC:

$$\text{BIC}_K = \ell_K(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) - \frac{\nu_K}{2}\log(n)$$

with $\nu_K$ the free parameter count. In the football application, BIC peaks sharply at $K=4$ (−264,121 against −264,383 for $K=3$ and −264,141 for $K=5$).

BIC has an asymptotic Bayesian justification as an approximation to the marginal likelihood, but its regularity conditions do not strictly hold for mixtures — it is used pragmatically rather than because the theory guarantees it. Alternatives include the Integrated Completed Likelihood (ICL), which additionally penalises entropy in the assignments and so favours well-separated clusters.

## Identifiability and Label Switching

Mixtures are only ever identifiable **up to permutation of the component labels** — relabelling components gives an identical density. This is benign for clustering (the partition is unchanged) but must be handled in Bayesian estimation, where MCMC chains can switch labels mid-run.

Beyond label switching, genuine identifiability requires conditions on the components. See [[identifiability]].

## Multi-Start and Local Optima

The mixture likelihood is typically multimodal, and EM converges only to a local optimum. Practice is therefore to run many random initialisations and keep the best: Amezouwui et al. use 1,000 starts, refine the best 100 after 10 iterations, then run 500 more.

This is not a minor implementation detail — reported mixture results are conditional on the search having found a good mode, and single-start results are unreliable.

## Uses Beyond Clustering

- **Density estimation** — a mixture of Gaussians approximates arbitrary smooth densities.
- **Latent class analysis** — mixtures of categorical distributions for survey data.
- **Robust modelling** — a wide component absorbs outliers.
- **Hidden Markov models** — a mixture where the latent indicator follows a [[markov-game|Markov chain]] rather than being i.i.d.

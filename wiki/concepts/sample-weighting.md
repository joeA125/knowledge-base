# Sample Weighting

Multiplying each training instance's loss by a weight, so that the fitted model reflects a target population rather than the population the data happens to contain. A small technique with an outsized effect when the data is unevenly generated by identifiable groups.

## The Problem in Event Data

Sports [[event-stream-data|event data]] is not a random sample of football actions. It is a census of actions, and **actions are not evenly distributed across players**. Elite attackers take far more shots than everyone else; centre-backs contest far more aerial duels.

An unweighted model therefore fits the best-represented players preferentially. For an [[expected-goals|xG]] model this is actively harmful, because the goal is an *average-player* model — a description of chance quality that is independent of who is shooting. If Messi's shots dominate the training set, the model partly learns Messi's finishing and reports it as chance quality, defeating the metric's purpose.

## The Correction

[[andrei-shelopugin|Shelopugin]] divides each instance's loss by the number of times that player appears in the training set. For classification:

$$\ell_i = \frac{1}{|\,p_i \in D\,|}\left[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right]$$

and analogously with squared error for regression targets. Implemented as a custom `objective` in [[gradient-boosting|LightGBM]].

The effect is that every *player* contributes equally to the fit, rather than every *event*. A player with 500 shots and one with 5 exert the same total influence.

Applied in three places in the paper: the xG models, the duel-context model behind [[duel-skill-rating]], and the [[expected-possession-value|EPV]] models themselves.

## What the Weight Should Be

The choice of $1/n_i$ is not neutral, and it is worth being explicit about what it assumes.

Full inverse-frequency weighting targets a **uniform-over-players** population — every footballer equally represented regardless of how much they play. That is the right target for an average-player situational model. It is the *wrong* target if the question is "what is the expected value of this situation in real football", where frequently-occurring players genuinely constitute more of the phenomenon.

Intermediate weightings ($1/\sqrt{n_i}$, or capping the weight) trade the two off and are common in practice. The paper does not discuss the choice or test alternatives.

There is also a variance cost. Upweighting rare players amplifies their noise; a player with three shots now carries the weight of one with three hundred, and the fitted model absorbs the sampling error in those three. Inverse-frequency weighting reduces bias and increases variance, which is the usual shape of this trade.

## Relation to Selection Bias

Reweighting is one of the standard remedies for [[selection-bias]] — but only for the tractable case where the target distribution is *known* and the observed units are a reweightable version of it.

It cannot fix the harder case, where units are missing entirely because of their outcomes. No weight applied to observed transfers recovers the transfers that never happened; that requires modelling the selection mechanism. See [[positive-unlabeled-learning]] for the version of the problem in this same paper that weighting does not solve.

The distinction is worth holding onto: **reweighting fixes composition, not absence.**
